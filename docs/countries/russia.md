# Russia — 1950

### *Союз нерушимый?* · The Union That Would Not Die

> *"They told the world we were finished in the autumn of '41. We have been finishing ever since.
> A state can lose its capital, its army, its leader, even its name — and still, in some frozen
> room beyond the Urals, refuse to die. That refusal is the only government we have left."*
> — attributed to a Red Army staff officer, Novosibirsk, winter 1949

## I. The State That Would Not Die

When the swastika rose over the Kremlin on the last day of September 1941, the world wrote the
obituary of the Soviet Union. It was wrong, but only just. The state did not surrender — it fled.
The government, the surviving institutes, the rolling-stock of half an economy, and millions of
people poured eastward ahead of the panzers, first to Kuibyshev, then, as the front ground to the
Volga and the Caucasus through 1942, on again beyond the Urals into the Siberian deep. By the time
the line froze on the planners' old **A–A line** — Archangel to Astrakhan — in 1943, what remained
of the USSR was a continent-sized rump: poor, beheaded, half-starved, and alive.

That is the first fact of Russia in 1950, and the most important one. **Everything west of the
Volga is gone** — annexed into the Reichskommissariats, given over to the SS and *Generalplan Ost*,
its cities renamed, its people worked to death or driven off. The Russia that survives is the
Russia of the rear: the Urals forges, the Kuznetsk coal, the cities that the evacuation trains
built into something more than they had ever been, the steppe and cotton republics of Central Asia,
and the cold immensity of Siberia. It has lost its heartland, its breadbasket, two-thirds of its
people, and the better part of its myth. What it has kept is distance, stubbornness, and a grievance
without a bottom.

This is not a confident socialist fatherland biding its time. It is a wounded animal that has
survived being left for dead, and has spent nine years deciding that the men who skinned it will
answer for it.

## II. From Catastrophe to Junta — 1941 to 1950

The rump has been governed, in nine years, by three different kinds of state — and the way each one
fell tells you what Russia has become.

### The god-tsar's quiet end

**Joseph Stalin** did not long outlive the catastrophe. The loss of Moscow shattered the one thing
his rule could not do without: the myth of his infallibility. Through 1942 he grew more erratic and
more murderous, turning on the very commanders trying to save what was left, hunting traitors to
explain a defeat that was his own. He had become a danger to the survival of the state — and to the
men around him.

In 1943, the official record says, Stalin suffered a stroke and died. The official record is a
fiction that almost no one in the inner circle believes. The truth — an open secret among the few
hundred people who matter, and the rot at the heart of every faction's legitimacy — is that he was
**poisoned**, quietly, by the one man with the means, the motive and the nerve: the chief of his own
secret police. The god of the revolution was murdered in his sickbed by the NKVD, and the deed was
dressed as the will of providence.

### Six years of the secret police

The man who killed him took his place. **Lavrentiy Beria** did not seize a throne; he simply *was*
the state, because by 1943 the NKVD was the only institution that still functioned — the only one
that could feed the cities, move the labour, hold the line behind the line, and keep a beheaded
nation from flying apart. For six years Beria ruled the rump as a pure terror-machine. The Party,
the **KPSS**, survived as a hollow shell, its congresses unheld, its doctrine recited over a state
that had quietly become a police apparatus with a flag. To the outside world — and to communist
America, which armed and funded him — Beria's rump *was* the Soviet Union, the surviving homeland of
the revolution, the western anchor of the red cause.

It held. It also rotted. By the late 1940s the secret-police state had bled the Red Army of every
liberty and most of its dignity, shot or imprisoned a generation of officers on suspicion, and ruled
the men who had actually saved the country as though they were the enemy. The army endured it
because there was a war to survive. When the war's edge dulled into the long, watchful peace, the
army stopped enduring it.

### The one night

In the winter of **1949–50**, the soldiers moved. Marshal **Georgy Zhukov** — the man who had
organised the defence of the Urals when everything west of them was falling, the closest thing the
rump had to a living hero — and the senior field commanders acted together, in a single coordinated
night. Garrisons seized the ministries; armoured columns ringed the NKVD compounds; and across the
rump, in a few hours of telephone calls and kicked-in doors, the **entire secret-police and Party
elite was arrested at once**. Beria was taken in his bed, as Stalin had been — and, unlike Stalin,
he was given a trial, of sorts, before he was shot.

It was over almost before the country knew it had begun. The men who had ruled by terror for six
years were in cells or in graves by morning, and the **Red Army held the state**.

### The hinge

That is where 1950 begins: **on the hinge, days or weeks after the knife fell.** Zhukov chairs a
**Provisional Military Council** — a junta in everything but the word — that rules by decree and
promises, repeatedly and publicly, to **convene a congress and hold elections** to give Russia a
legitimate government at last. Whether those elections are ever genuinely held, what they are
allowed to produce, and whether the men who hold the guns will hand power to anyone — these are the
open questions on which the whole future of the state, and its place in the world, now turn. The
knife has fallen. The future is unwritten.

## III. The General and the Vacuum

The junta is not a movement. It is a handful of men holding a country at gunpoint and arguing, in
private, about what to do with it. The factions that matter:

- **The soldiers' centre — Zhukov and the high command.** Not ideologues but pragmatists: men who
  saved the state and now mean to rebuild it, husband its strength, and decide its fate on their own
  terms rather than Moscow-orthodoxy's or Washington's. Zhukov genuinely seems to intend the promised
  elections — but a soldier's instinct for order, and a justified fear of what a free vote might
  unleash in a starving, vengeful country, pulls the other way every single day.
- **The Party loyalists — the keepers of the flame.** The surviving orthodox communists, humiliated
  by the army that shot their police chief, who believe salvation lies in *restoring* the revolution:
  a proper Party, proper doctrine, and reunion with the red bloc abroad. They are the rump's bridge to
  communist America, and they want the elections to hand power back to the Party that the soldiers took
  it from.
- **The drowned men — the NKVD remnant.** Beria is dead, but a secret-police apparatus six years deep
  does not vanish in one night. Its survivors are scattered, hunted, and very dangerous — a knife in
  the dark that the junta can neither fully see nor fully destroy, and a standing temptation for any
  faction willing to use it.
- **The reformers and the young.** A thin, hopeful current — economists, front-line officers, the
  generation with no stake in the old terror — who take the promise of elections at face value and
  want a Russia that is genuinely *new*: a constitutional state, even a democratic one, that has earned
  the right to be ruled by consent rather than fear.

None of these can govern alone. Together, around the empty space where a legitimate government should
be, they are the materials of either a rebirth or a second civil war.

## IV. The Forge Beyond the Urals

Russia is desperately poor and surprisingly armed, and the paradox is the work of the evacuation.
When the factories of the west fled east in 1941–42, they did not all die on the rails — a great
many of them were rebuilt, brutally, in the Urals and western Siberia, and around them grew the only
modern economy the rump still owns:

- **The Urals arsenal** — Magnitogorsk's iron, Chelyabinsk's tank works (*Tankograd*), the machine
  shops of Sverdlovsk: the forge that out-built no one any more, but still casts the steel of a
  rebuilding army.
- **The Kuznetsk basin** — the coal and metal of western Siberia, the rump's industrial spine.
- **Soviet Central Asia** — the Kazakh metals and grain and the cotton republics that feed and clothe
  what is left of the Union, and the ores and manpower that any rebuilding of the army must draw upon.
- **The relocated institutes** — the scientists and design bureaus carried east ahead of the front:
  the rump's true crown jewels, the "Soviet science" the whole red bloc counts on to close the arms
  gap with the Reich.

It is, even so, a poverty economy. The consumer goods do not come; the rationing never ends; whole
districts live in the half-built barracks of the evacuation a decade on. For six years the gap was
filled by **Red American aid** — the industry of the new red superpower married to the rump's brains
and ore. That lifeline is exactly what the coup has thrown into doubt: a military junta of uncertain
colour is not the orthodox communist client Washington was bankrolling, and the matériel has slowed
to a wary trickle while the bloc waits to see what Russia is about to become.

## V. The Red Bloc and the Complicated Friend

For most of the rump's existence, its place in the world was simple: it was the wounded heart of
world communism, the senior partner in spirit and the junior in everything else — the homeland of
the revolution that supplied the bloc its legitimacy, its manpower and its brains, while
**communist America** supplied the industry and the money. That marriage of *Soviet science and
Soviet numbers to American factories* is the engine that, left to run, would reforge the Red Army
into a force able to challenge the Reich across the Volga. It is the single most important
relationship Russia has, and the road to everything that comes after runs through it.

**The coup has put a question mark over all of it.** Communist America does not know what to make of
a Russia where the generals have shot the Party's police chief and talk of holding elections. Is this
still a comrade, or a Bonapartist deviation dressed in red — a counter-revolution that happens to use
the old flag? Moscow-orthodoxy is the language Washington trusts; Zhukov speaks the language of the
barracks. So the bloc's stance is a tangle of need and suspicion: it **needs** Russia (the western
front against the Reich, the homeland's prestige, and the manpower and brains without which the bloc
cannot match the Reich's armies), and it **distrusts** the junta that now runs it. Quietly, the red superpower
courts the Party loyalists, hoping the promised elections will hand Russia back to people it
understands.

Which means the bloc tie is not a fact but a **prize** — the thing the rump's internal struggle will
decide:

- **If the loyalists are restored**, Russia re-reds, the marriage is consummated, the rearmament
  proceeds apace, and Russia takes its canonical place as the revanchist western jaw of the communist bloc.
  *This is the baseline future the rest of the setting assumes.*
- **If Zhukov's soldiers keep power** as a "Russia-first" military-nationalist state, the rump drifts
  out of the bloc's orbit — non-aligned, or even an enemy of the very communism it was born from.
- **If genuine elections produce a constitutional Russia**, the break is deeper still, and the map of
  the coming war is redrawn.

Russia in 1950 has not yet chosen. Every power that matters is watching to see which Russia walks out
of the junta's promised election — because the answer decides whether the red bloc has two jaws or one.

## VI. Three Frontiers

The rump is squeezed, and it knows it.

- **The Volga — Germany.** The western frontier is the wound that defines the nation: across the river
  lie the lost cities, the murdered millions of *Generalplan Ost*, the renamed homeland. Every Russian
  faction, whatever else divides them, looks west with the same hunger. The rump cannot hope to match
  the Reich in open battle — yet. But the SS empire in the East is a powder-keg of starving, brutalised
  people, and the rump's cheapest weapon is a box of rifles smuggled to the partisans in the forests of
  Muscovy and Ukraine. The revanche begins, when it begins, in the trees.
- **The Pacific fringe — Japan.** The eastern knife. As the Union collapsed, Japan helped itself to the
  vulnerable edge of the Soviet Far East — **Sakhalin, Kamchatka, and the Maritime coast** — pressing
  the rump back off all but a **precarious northern toehold** on the Pacific along the notional
  German–Japanese demarcation line. It is a second front the rump can least afford, a permanent reminder
  that it is encircled, and a standing danger that the other victor of the war could lunge again while
  Russia's eyes are fixed on the Volga.
- **The soft south — Central Asia.** The grain, the cotton and the metals of the southern republics are
  the rump's larder and the raw material of its rebuilding — and its underbelly: distant, restless, and
  held by a state with very little left to hold it with.

## VII. The Long Game — to 1955

Russia's strategic problem reduces to a single argument, and it is the argument that splits the junta:

**Rebuild, or strike?**

- The **Rebuild** case: the rump is weak, encircled and broke. To move now is to be crushed now. Better
  to husband strength in secret, reforge the army, let Germany's coming succession crisis do the work of
  weakening the enemy, and choose the hour of the reckoning rather than have it chosen for you.
- The **Strike** case: the Reich will never be more divided than in the chaos of its dying Führer's
  succession, and Russia will never be less likely to be left in peace. Probe the Volga, arm the East,
  and lunge before Germany resolves itself — because a rump that waits forever is a rump that is
  colonised at leisure.

On the canonical path — the one where the loyalists win and Russia re-reds — both arguments end in the
same place. Through the early 1950s American industry and the rump's manpower reforge the Red Army into
a force that can, at last, contemplate the river, and the bloc never for a day accepts the map. The
provocations escalate — border incidents on the Volga, "volunteer" formations, a partisan war in the
Reichskommissariats fanned from the east — until one of them is allowed to run out of control. In 1955
the **Volga incident** — a clash on the river the revanchists engineer and refuse to let cool —
converges with America's pressure on Britain and the Atlantic into the general reckoning the whole
*Pax Germanica* scenario is built toward: the war in which the rump that would not die comes back across
the river for everything it lost.

The divergent paths bend that road. A Bonapartist or democratic Russia outside the bloc fights a
different war, or sits out the one the reds were counting on it to start. Which is precisely why every
capital — Berlin, Washington, and the rump's own — cares so much who wins the junta's promised election.

---

## VIII. The Armed Forces

Russia in 1950 fields an army rebuilt from ruin, and very little else.

- **The Red Army** — the only institution that works, the maker of the junta, and the instrument of
  whatever comes next. Battered and purged, far smaller than the colossus of 1941, but hard, experienced,
  and rearming as fast as a poverty economy and a wary patron will allow. It is Zhukov's power and
  Russia's one real card.
- **The Air Force** — thin and ageing, a fraction of what the front demands, dependent on Red American
  designs and goodwill for any hope of closing the gap with the Luftwaffe's jets.
- **The Navy** — all but nonexistent; a landlocked rump with one icy, exposed toehold on the Pacific has
  little use and less means for a fleet.
- **The partisan instrument** — the cheapest and, for now, the most useful weapon in the arsenal: rifles,
  radios and cadres smuggled west to keep the SS empire bleeding.
- **The long game** — the reason for the patience: American factories married to Soviet numbers and
  science, slowly reforging the Red Army into a force that could one day contest the Volga.

### Designer's notes

- **Russia is written as the deliberate counterpoint to Germany.** Both nations face a leadership crisis
  at the heart of the setting — but at *opposite phases*. Germany's is a **deathbed succession that has
  not yet broken** (a dying Führer, an open question). Russia's is a **coup that has just broken** (the
  knife already fallen, the future racing to be written). One scenario is about who inherits; the other
  is about what is built on the ruins. They rhyme without repeating.
- **The start date sits on the hinge**, and the coup, the junta and the promised election are intended as
  a **decision-driven chain** in the eventual game files: consolidate the coup, hunt the NKVD remnant, set
  the junta's character, and steer toward — or quietly strangle — the elections, with each branch
  resolving Russia's ideology and its place in the world.
- **Canon reconciliation.** [The Road to Victory](../world/the-road-to-victory.md) and
  [germany.md](germany.md) treat Russia as part of the communist bloc, sharing in its rearmament and
  marching toward the 1955 war. This document honours that as the **canonical, loyalist-restoration
  default** and explains *how a non-communist junta gets back to it* — rather than contradicting it. The
  Bonapartist and democratic outcomes are explicit **player divergences**; the world and Germany docs
  remain authoritative wherever they speak.
- **Plausibility caveats, kept honest.** That a beheaded Soviet rump survives at all is the load-bearing
  assumption — defended by sheer distance, the depth of the 1941–42 industrial evacuation, and the
  Germans' refusal to die of logistics chasing it into Siberia. The **Beria-poisons-Stalin** and the
  **one-night army coup against the secret police** are deliberate echoes of the real events of 1953,
  moved earlier and given to the army — the cleanest in-world route from a Stalinist terror-state to a
  Zhukov junta.
- **Real historical figures are used for the "a reality that might have happened" feel** (Stalin, Beria,
  Zhukov, Konev, Molotov, Kurchatov); lower-tier and fictional roles can be freely renamed, and the
  scaffold names can be mapped onto these parts if invented names are preferred.
- **Inspirations:** *Fatherland*, *The Man in the High Castle*, *SS-GB*, and the faction-driven,
  instability-under-the-veneer tone of *Kaiserreich (Redux)*.
- **Hooks for sibling documents** (`docs/countries/`): **Germany** (the Reich across the Volga, and the
  succession crisis Russia is waiting on), **America** (the communist superpower that is Russia's
  complicated patron and the industrial half of the bloc's rearmament), **Japan** (the eastern knife on the Pacific
  fringe), **Britain** (the Atlantic flank the bloc means to crack), and **Italy** (the Mediterranean
  partner of the enemy).
