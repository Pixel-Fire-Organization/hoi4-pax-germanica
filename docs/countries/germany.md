# The German Reich — 1950

### *Großdeutsches Reich* · The Heart of the New Order

> *"We were promised the thousand-year Reich. No one told us the first decade would be the
> hardest. We won the war, and now we cannot afford the peace."*
> — attributed to a Reich Ministry of Economics official, intercepted in a private letter, 1949

## I. From Victory to Vigil

Germany won the war in the East before the snows of 1941 and finished it everywhere else by 1943.
The story of how — the drive on Moscow that did not turn south, the fall of the Soviet capital,
the British peace that flipped the world, the separate peace that left America to face Japan alone — is told in
[The Road to Victory](../world/the-road-to-victory.md). What matters for Germany in 1950 is the
*shape* of what was won, and the price of holding it.

By the proclamation of the New Order in 1945, Germany was the unchallenged master of the European
continent: from the Atlantic coast of conquered France to the banks of the Volga, from the North
Cape to the Sahara through its allies and clients. No power on earth could invade it. And yet
within five years the mood in Berlin had curdled from triumph into a kind of permanent, anxious
vigil — the sense of a garrison holding a fortress far too large for it, against enemies who have
not been destroyed but only pushed beyond the horizon, and against a clock that no one can stop:
the slow failing of the Führer himself.

This is the central fact of Germany in 1950. It is not a confident empire enjoying its victory.
It is an overextended, exhausted, brilliantly armed state that has begun, very quietly, to be
afraid.

## II. The New Order — 1945–1950

### The Greater Reich and its dependencies

The territory Germany controls falls into three rings, and the difference between them governs
everything.

- **The Reich proper** — the *Großdeutsches Reich*. Germany within its 1939 borders plus the
  outright annexations: Austria, the Sudetenland and the Czech lands as the Protectorate, the
  incorporated western half of Poland, Alsace-Lorraine, Luxembourg, Eupen-Malmedy, western Slovenia,
  Danzig, the annexed Baltic coast. Here the law of the Reich runs and the population is German or
  being made German. This is the core that must be kept content at any cost.
- **The European clients** — a ring of satellite, allied and occupied states bound into a
  German-led economic bloc, the *Großraumwirtschaft*: a humiliated, collaborationist France; the
  Low Countries under occupation; a constellation of allied dictatorships across the Balkans and the
  north; and **Italy**, the senior partner of the original Axis, lord of the Mediterranean and North
  Africa, prickly and proud and increasingly a rival rather than a subordinate. These states keep a
  shadow of sovereignty; Berlin sets their tariffs, their currencies and their foreign policy.
- **The colonial East** — the **Reichskommissariats**: Ostland (the Baltics and Belarus), Ukraine,
  Muscovy (*Moskowien*), and the Caucasus, running to the Volga. This is not occupation in any
  European sense. It is colonisation under the SS, governed by **Generalplan Ost** — the programme
  to clear, starve and resettle the East and turn it into German living space. It is the engine of
  the Reich's labour and food supply, the showpiece of the regime's ideology, and an open,
  bleeding wound that has not healed for nine years.

### The economy that cannot stand down

The defining feature of the German economy in 1950 is captured in a single phrase the regime uses
with pride and the planners use with dread: the **total-war economy**. The factories that out-built
the Soviet Union were never reconverted to peace. There are reasons given — the need to garrison a
continent, the megaprojects, the rearmament of the next generation of weapons — but the truest
reason is structural: a system built for total war does not know how to do anything else, and the
men who run it fear what idle workers and disappointed expectations might do.

So the output pours on. The rebuilding of Berlin as **Germania**, a monstrous capital of marble and
megalomania. The autobahn network driven east toward the Urals. The Atlantic Wall maintained against
an invasion that will never come. A navy and an air force and an army kept at a size no peace could
justify. And underneath all of it, holding it up, the thing the propaganda never shows: the **forced
labour** of tens of millions — *Ostarbeiter* from the conquered East, prisoners, the inmates of a
camp system that did not close when the war ended but became a permanent fixture of the economy.

The result is a colossus with a hollow core. German military industry is the most advanced on earth.
German consumers, nearly a decade after victory, still queue for goods that the regime keeps
promising and the planners keep diverting. The gap between the promised prosperity of the New Order
and its grey, rationed reality is the single most dangerous fact in domestic politics — and everyone
in the leadership knows it.

### Society and dissent

On the surface, Germany in 1950 is the most ordered society on earth: one party, one Führer, a
population drilled into the rituals of the New Order from the cradle. Beneath the surface it is
quietly fracturing along familiar lines.

- A **conservative-nationalist** establishment — much of the officer corps, the old civil service,
  the industrialists — that backed Hitler for victory and now wants the war economy wound down, the
  empire rationalised, and Germany allowed to become a normal great power. They are the natural
  constituency of the "Pax Germanica" tendency.
- A **democratic and liberal underground** — small, hunted, but real — kept alive by the memory of
  what Germany was before 1933 and fed, increasingly, by clandestine contact with the outside world.
- A **communist underground** that the regime swore it had exterminated, reviving in the factories
  and the labour camps, and now linked by money and propaganda to the revanchist powers beyond the
  Volga and across the Atlantic.
- A **disaffected youth** — the first generation with no memory of the struggle, bored by the
  rituals, drawn to forbidden music and forbidden questions — that the Party finds it cannot fully
  command.

None of these threatens the regime on its own. Together, in a moment of crisis — say, the death of
the only Führer the nation has ever known — they are the kindling for something the Gestapo cannot
fully see and does not know how to put out.

## III. The Failing Führer and the Struggle for the Succession

### The man at the Berghof

**Adolf Hitler** is alive in 1950, and that is the problem.

He is sixty years old and visibly failing. The tremor in his left hand — the Parkinson's that the
regime denies and the whole leadership can see — has worsened year on year. He is withdrawn to the
Berghof for months at a time, seeing fewer and fewer people, his judgement erratic, his moods
darkening. He still issues the occasional thunderbolt that reminds everyone who is master, and he
remains the sole, untransferable source of the regime's legitimacy: every official, every soldier,
every functionary swore a personal oath to *him*, not to an office or a constitution. But he no
longer governs. The day-to-day rule of the greatest empire on earth has slipped, quietly, into
other hands — and every one of those hands is reaching for what will be left when he dies.

There is no mechanism for the succession. There is a paper decree from 1941 naming Göring, which no
one any longer takes seriously, and beyond that, nothing — only a set of power blocs circling a
deathbed.

### The paladins

- **Reichsleiter Martin Bormann** — Head of the Party Chancellery and Secretary to the Führer; the
  "Brown Eminence." Bormann's power is entirely derivative and therefore entirely urgent: he controls
  *access* to Hitler, and signs the decrees that issue in Hitler's name, and so for now he is the
  closest thing the Reich has to a regent. But the instant Hitler dies, the source of his authority
  dies with him. No one has a stronger motive to manage the succession — and to make himself
  indispensable to whoever wins it.
- **Reichsführer-SS Heinrich Himmler** — the most powerful institutional figure in the Reich after
  the Führer. The SS is a state within the state: the Gestapo, the Waffen-SS, the police, the camps,
  and above all the **entire colonial East**, which is his personal empire. Himmler is the standard-
  bearer of the **Eternal Reich** tendency — permanent racial struggle, no demobilisation, the
  conquest of the East completed by blood, the revanchist enemies crushed before they can recover. He
  is also feared and loathed by nearly everyone else, because an SS succession means an SS state.
- **Reichsminister Albert Speer** — the architect of the war economy and the voice of the
  technocrats and industrialists. Speer can read a ledger, and the ledger terrifies him: he knows
  the total-war economy is devouring its own foundations and that the empire cannot be held by force
  forever. He is the natural leader of the **Pax Germanica** tendency — rationalise, partially
  demobilise, consolidate what can be held and let go of what cannot, buy peace and time, perhaps
  even reach an accommodation with the communist powers. Pragmatism dressed as statesmanship.
- **The Wehrmacht** — the professional officer corps, personified by its senior field marshals, that
  has spent fifteen years resenting the rise of the SS and would dearly love to see the army restored
  to primacy. It is not a faction so much as a **kingmaker**: in any real contest for the succession,
  the side the army backs will likely win, and the side it refuses will likely fall.
- **Reichsmarschall Hermann Göring** — once the designated heir, now a bloated, drug-addled relic
  whom no serious person wants in command but whose formal claim and residual prestige make him a
  wildcard that the others must still account for.

### The fork in the road

The struggle between these blocs is not yet open — Hitler still lives, and while he lives no one
dares move. But its shape is already set, and it maps onto a single question that will define the
Reich's future: **does Germany consolidate, or does it remain forever at war?**

- The **Pax Germanica** path (Speer, much of the army, the old establishment) means treating the
  victory as complete: wind down the war economy, settle the empire, seek a cold peace with the
  communist bloc, and let Germany become a stable, dominant, *normal* great power. Its risk is that
  consolidation looks like weakness — to the hardliners at home and the revanchists abroad.
- The **Eternal Reich** path (Himmler, the SS, the true believers) means treating the victory as
  merely a beginning: keep the nation mobilised forever, finish the conquest of the East, and strike
  the recovering enemies before they are strong enough to strike back. Its risk is that perpetual war
  is precisely what the exhausted, overstretched Reich can least afford.

Hitler's death — whenever it comes, and everyone believes it is coming soon — will detonate this
choice. **Germany's central crisis in 1950 is that it must decide what it is, at the exact moment it
is least able to decide anything.**

## IV. A World of Three Empires — 1950

Germany is the greatest of the powers but no longer the only one that matters. The 1950 world is
**tripolar**, with a dying fourth corner that will not stay dead. *(Each is detailed in its own
document; what follows is only how the world looks from Berlin.)*

- **Japan** — the other victor. Master of East Asia and the Pacific, enriched beyond measure by the
  spoils of the war it won on its own. To Berlin, Japan is a co-belligerent that has become a
  **rival**: the two empires never had to share a border until they had conquered everything between
  them, and now they grate against one another over the carcass of Asia — the line of demarcation in
  Siberia, and the oil of the German-held Near East, which Japan covets and the Reich will not share.
  In the coming crisis, no one in Berlin is certain whether Japan will stand with Germany, stay aloof,
  or take its cut while the Reich is distracted.
- **Communist America** — the great surprise of the age and the object of German bewilderment.
  Broken in the Pacific and betrayed by Britain, the United States tore itself apart in the late
  1940s and emerged a **red superpower**: diminished by its Pacific losses and by a breakaway
  remnant, ideologically aflame, and consumed by the conviction that the map of 1945 is an
  injustice to be overturned. It is the arsenal and the paymaster of every enemy the Reich has.
- **Rump Soviet Russia** — beyond the Volga and the Urals, the Soviet state that Germany broke but
  failed to kill clings to life in the Siberian vastness: poor, paranoid, rebuilding in secret, and
  staring west across the river at every city, mine and field it lost. It is weak. It is also
  patient, and it is being armed.
- **Britain** — Germany's strange, sullen client on its western flank. The 1941 Understanding cost
  Britain its Mediterranean and African empire and left it, in American eyes, the power that quit the
  war and abandoned its own kin to Japan — but the bargain has rotted from within. By 1950 the British
  Isles are split three ways — between **Lord Halifax's** ageing collaborationist government, the
  restored and unloved **King Edward VIII**, and a growing **democratic resistance** that damns the
  peace, the king, and the shameful abandonment of America alike. The white Dominions remain loyal to
  London but restive, appalled by the terms. A Britain that fractures could open the Atlantic door for
  the communist powers — which is exactly what they intend.
- **Italy** — the senior junior partner, **lord of the Mediterranean**, its empire fattened on British
  and French spoils — Malta, Cyprus, Somaliland, Algeria and Tunisia — proud and restive, neither
  wholly an ally nor wholly a subordinate, watching Germany's leadership crisis with interest.
- **Spain** — the last to join and the least of the victors, but a victor nonetheless: Franco held
  back until Moscow fell, then entered the war to take **Gibraltar** and complete his hold on
  **Morocco**. A cautious, cash-poor junior client, jealous of its winnings and wary of the next war.

Between these powers there is a peace, of a kind. It is the peace of exhaustion — not of trust. And
it is running out.

## V. The Gathering Storm — 1950 to 1955

The détente of the early 1950s is a holding pattern, and three forces are converging to break it.

**1. The economy must choose.** The total-war economy cannot run forever, but it cannot simply stop
either — to demobilise is to risk the unemployment and disappointment that the regime most fears, and
to admit that the long emergency is over. A state organised for war tends, in the end, to find one.

**2. The succession will break — and then reset the board.** Hitler's death, when it comes (and few
expect him to see 1953), detonates the contest between Speer's consolidation and Himmler's eternal war.
Whichever way it falls, the *transition itself* is a moment of profound weakness — a contested
succession, perhaps an open struggle between the SS and the army — that the Reich survives but does not
soon forget. By the time a new master has secured the state, years have been lost and the empire's
mortality has been shown to every enemy watching. The communist powers are counting on the window that
opens on the far side of it.

**3. The communist bloc is rearming — and has never accepted the map.** Red America's industry,
married to what survives of Soviet science and the manpower of the rump beyond the Urals, spends the
early 1950s rebuilding the strength the war took from it. The revanchist powers do not believe the
peace of 1945 was ever anything but a truce, and they will not sit still while a Reich that has just
weathered its succession turns a decade of consolidation into renewed strength. Better to strike, the
planners in the red capitals reason, in the narrow window while the new German leadership is still
finding its feet and the enemy's attention is fixed inward.

So the provocations begin, and escalate, toward a reckoning in **1955**:

- **The Volga front.** Rump Russia, rearmed and emboldened, probes west across the river — border
  incidents, "volunteer" formations, support for the partisans — testing whether the overstretched
  Reich will fight for the far edge of its empire. It is here that the shooting starts: the **Volga
  incident** of 1955 — a border clash the revanchists engineer and refuse to let cool — is the spark
  that lights the general war.
- **The Atlantic and Britain.** Communist America strikes where Germany is softest: at the sea lanes,
  and at Britain. American money and arms pour into the British democratic resistance, aiming to crack
  the Reich's western client wide open and turn the British Isles from a shield into a dagger.
- **The partisan East.** The smouldering insurgency in the Reichskommissariats, fanned by foreign
  arms and the scent of German weakness, threatens to flare from a chronic wound into a continental
  uprising — the exact nightmare the Eternal Reich faction has always predicted.
- **The Japanese question.** And over all of it hangs the uncertainty of Japan — whether the Reich's
  fellow victor will hold the line in the East or pursue its own advantage while Germany bleeds.

This is the war the *Pax Germanica* scenario builds toward: not a German war of conquest, but a
**war of survival for the New Order** — a divided, exhausted, brilliantly armed Reich, its founder
dead and its new leadership scarcely settled, forced to fight for everything it took, against enemies who have spent a decade
deciding that the peace of 1945 was only an intermission.

## VI. The Armed Forces

Germany in 1950 fields the most powerful military on earth — and one stretched perilously thin.

- **The Heer (Army)** — vast, battle-hardened, and the world's leading practitioner of mechanised
  war, but committed to garrisoning a continent: an enormous fraction of its strength is tied down on
  the Volga front and in anti-partisan duty across the East rather than concentrated for a war.
- **The Luftwaffe** — technologically dominant, the first air force into the jet age, but ageing in
  its leadership under the discredited Göring and uncertain in its priorities.
- **The Kriegsmarine** — rebuilt around the U-boat arm that won the Atlantic, and now the Reich's
  thinnest service relative to its task: the German surface fleet is no match for the combined naval
  weight a hostile Britain and a red America could bring to bear if the western flank collapses.
- **The Waffen-SS** — Himmler's army-within-the-army, ideologically reliable and increasingly
  well-equipped, and a standing political threat to the Wehrmacht's primacy.

For all its might, this is a military built to hold, not to fight — a garrison force dispersed across
a continent it cannot demobilise from and cannot fully secure. Its edge is technological and
professional; its weakness is arithmetic. Germany has no ally it trusts and enemies on every horizon,
and the peace it keeps rests not on any deterrent but on the sheer exhaustion of a world that fought
itself to a standstill in 1945. When that exhaustion lifts — as it is lifting — the Reich will have to
fight for its sprawling empire with an army already stretched to its limits.

### Designer's notes

- **Germany is written as the *strained* hegemon, not the triumphant one** — per the brief, a power
  "struggling with recovery and leadership challenges." The drama is internal (the succession, the
  economy, the dissent) as much as external (the rearming communist bloc). This keeps the start date
  tense rather than a victory lap, and gives the focus tree somewhere to go.
- **The Pax Germanica vs Eternal Reich fork is the spine.** It maps cleanly onto real power blocs —
  Speer's technocrats vs Himmler's SS, with Bormann holding the ring and the Wehrmacht as kingmaker —
  so the player's central choice has faces and consequences.
- **Real historical figures are used where the brief already used them** (Hitler, Halifax, Edward
  VIII), for the "a reality that might have happened" feel; lower-tier and fictional roles can be
  freely renamed. The mod's scaffold names (Reinhardt, Faber, Vogt, Adler, Stein) are *not* canon and
  can be mapped onto these roles if the user prefers invented names.
- **The 1955 war is set up but not depicted** — its trigger (the **Volga incident**) and its drivers
  (the resolved-but-costly succession + a rearmed communist bloc that never accepted the map) are
  seeded here so the scenario's mid-game has a destination.
- **Inspirations:** *Fatherland*, *The Man in the High Castle*, *SS-GB*, *Kaiserreich (Redux)*.
- **Hooks for sibling documents** (`docs/countries/`): **Japan** (the rival victor; its stance in
  the coming war), **America** (the Second Civil War; the communist revolution; the territorial
  losses), **Russia** (the rump beyond the Urals; the covert rearmament), **Britain** (the three-way
  schism: Halifax / Edward VIII / the resistance), and **Italy** (the restive Mediterranean partner).
